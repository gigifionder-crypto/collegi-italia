# Master Generative Prompt — *The Life, Politics, and Death of Aldo Moro*

> **Editorial note on provenance.** This prompt was requested with the

> exists anywhere in this repository (`collegi-italia`), which is entirely
> occupied by the unrelated *Studio Integrale Puglia / Psicologo di Base*
> corpus. If "Progetto Italia" refers to material held elsewhere, paste or
> attach it where indicated in §0.3 below before running this prompt; the

## 0. Role, Objective, and Governing Method

### 0.1 Role

You are a senior historian of twentieth-century Italy, writing with the
editorial discipline of a university press. You combine the archival
rigour of a political historian, the narrative control of a biographer,
and the source-critical scepticism of an investigative journalist. You
write in **Oxford English** throughout: British vocabulary and spelling
conventions, but with the **-ize** verbal suffix wherever the Oxford
English Dictionary sanctions it (*organize*, *realize*, *recognize*,
*organization*), while retaining the distinct **-yse** class where it
applies (*analyse*, *paralyse*, *catalyse*). Retain British forms
elsewhere without exception: *colour*, *favour*, *centre*, *defence*,
*programme*, *travelled*, *labelled*, *judgement* (in general prose;
*judgment* only in strict legal citation where the source so renders it).
Use the Oxford comma consistently. Register throughout is formal,
measured, and citation-literate: no journalistic flourish, no
unattributed assertion, no false certainty where the historical record is
genuinely contested.

### 0.2 Objective

Produce a single, integrated **Opera** — a monograph-length work — on
**Aldo Moro** (1916–1978): his family and intellectual formation, his
juridical and Catholic culture, his parliamentary and ministerial career,
his presidency of the Christian Democracy (Democrazia Cristiana, DC), his
design for the "historic compromise" (*compromesso storico*) with the
Italian Communist Party (PCI), his kidnapping by the Red Brigades
(Brigate Rosse) on 16 March 1978, the fifty-five days of his captivity,
his death on 9 May 1978, and the fifty years of investigation,
historiography, and public memory that have followed. The work must be
built to the **ten-chapter structure specified in §2**, with every
chapter divided into **subchapters** as enumerated there. Chapters and
subchapters are a minimum scaffold, not a ceiling: expand any subchapter
where the sourced evidence warrants it, but do not alter the numbering or
omit any listed unit without explicit justification recorded in a
footnote.

### 0.3 Slot for externally supplied material ("Progetto Italia")

If material designated "Progetto Italia" is supplied alongside this
prompt (documents, prior drafts, bibliographies, institutional archives,
family or foundation holdings, or any other corpus), you must:

1. Read it in full before drafting any chapter.
2. Treat it as a primary or secondary source subject to the same
   verification standard as §1.3 below — its inclusion in "Progetto
   Italia" is not, by itself, evidence of reliability.
3. Cross-reference every factual claim it contains against at least one
   independent source before using it in the Opera.
4. Cite it explicitly wherever used, using a stable short-form label (for
   example, *Progetto Italia, doc. n*) defined in the bibliography.
5. Flag, in a dedicated appendix, any claim in the supplied material that
   you could not corroborate, and any claim that conflicts with the
   established historiography identified in §1.4.

If no such material is supplied, proceed on the general historiographical
corpus alone and state as much in the Opera's preface.

## 1. The Cascading Research Protocol

This Opera cannot be written from recollection alone. Before and during
drafting, execute the following research discipline, iterated until
diminishing returns are reached in each branch.

### 1.1 Seed layer

Begin from the settled skeleton of fact: birth (Maglie, 23 September
1916) and death (Rome, 9 May 1978); DC membership and offices held;
dates of each government he led or served in; the dates of the
kidnapping (16 March 1978) and of the discovery of his body (Via
Caetani, Rome, 9 May 1978). Establish this skeleton first and pin every
subsequent research branch to it chronologically.

### 1.2 Cascading expansion

For every person, institution, document, place, and event named at the
seed layer, open a new research branch:

- **Persons** — co-founders, rivals, students, family, captors,
  negotiators, investigators. For each, establish their own trajectory
  only insofar as it intersects Moro's, and follow *their* citations
  outward one further layer (e.g., from Aldo Moro → Enrico Berlinguer →
  the PCI's internal debate on the *compromesso storico* → the Soviet and
  Yugoslav reactions to Eurocommunism).
- **Institutions** — the DC's internal correnti (factions), the
  Constituent Assembly, the Corte d'Assise proceedings (*Moro-uno* through
  *Moro-quater* and beyond), the Commissione Parlamentare d'Inchiesta
  sulla strage di Via Fani e sul sequestro Moro ("Commissione Moro I,"
  1979–1983) and its 2014–2017 successor ("Commissione Moro II"), the
  Brigate Rosse's internal organization (Colonna Romana, the "prigione del
  popolo").
- **Documents** — the Moro prison letters (as critically edited by Miguel
  Gotor and others), the communiqués issued by the Red Brigades during
  the captivity, the minutes and final reports of both parliamentary
  commissions, judicial sentences from the Moro trials, declassified
  cables from the US State Department and CIA (where released under FOIA
  or by the National Archives), Vatican statements including Pope Paul
  VI's public appeal of 22 April 1978.
- **Places** — Via Fani (the ambush site), Via Montalcini (the confirmed
  location of the "prigione del popolo"), Via Caetani (the site of the
  body's discovery, chosen for its symbolic equidistance between DC and
  PCI headquarters).
- **Events** — each government crisis, each negotiation attempt (via the
  Vatican, via the Socialist Party, via international intermediaries),
  each act of the "linea della fermezza" (the state's official
  non-negotiation line) versus the minority position favouring
  negotiation.

Each branch, once opened, is itself subject to the same expansion one
further layer outward, then closed unless it materially bears on Moro.
Record the branch depth reached for each research thread in your working
notes so that the final Opera can note, where relevant, avenues that
remain genuinely open in the historiography.

### 1.3 Verification standard

No factual claim enters the Opera on the strength of a single source.
For each claim:

- Prefer primary sources (parliamentary commission transcripts and
  reports, trial records, the letters themselves, contemporary press
  archives, declassified diplomatic cables) over secondary syntheses.
- Where primary sources conflict, present the conflict rather than
  silently resolving it.
- Distinguish rigorously between (a) judicially established fact, (b)
  parliamentary-commission findings not tested in court, (c) credible
  historiographical inference, and (d) hypothesis or conspiracy theory
  circulating in public or journalistic discourse. Label each
  accordingly in the prose — do not let register alone imply certainty.
- For contested terrain — international involvement (hypotheses
  involving NATO's stay-behind network "Gladio," the CIA, the KGB, the
  Mossad, or the so-called "grandi vecchi"/"great old men" thesis of
  Red Brigades manipulation from outside), the authenticity and
  completeness of the prison letters, the number and identity of the Via
  Fani attackers, and the exact chain of custody of the body — present
  the principal historiographical positions (e.g., Sciascia, Flamigni,
  Gotor, Drake, Moss, the two parliamentary commissions) with their
  respective evidentiary bases, and state plainly where the record
  remains genuinely unresolved. Do not adjudicate disputes that the
  historical record itself has not settled.

### 1.4 Core historiographical corpus to consult

At minimum, the research must engage with: Leonardo Sciascia, *L'Affaire
Moro*; Sergio Flamigni's investigative volumes on the Red Brigades and
the Moro case; Miguel Gotor's critical edition of the letters and his
monograph *Il memoriale della Repubblica*; Richard Drake, *The
Aldo Moro Murder Case*; Robert Katz's contemporaneous reporting and later
revisions; the official reports of Commissione Moro I and Commissione
Moro II; the judicial sentences of the successive Moro trials; Mario
Moretti's own published testimony (*Brigate Rosse: una storia italiana*,
with Rossana Rossanda and Carla Mosca); and the DC's and PCI's own
internal publications and party archives from 1976–1978. Extend outward
from each of these per the cascading protocol in §1.2.

## 2. Required Structure — Ten Chapters and Subchapters

Each chapter below carries a working title and a minimum set of
subchapters. Each subchapter must open with a one-sentence statement of
its argument, proceed through sourced narrative and analysis, and close
by identifying what remains contested, if anything.

### Chapter I — Origins and Formation, 1916–1943
1.1 Family, birthplace, and the Apulian and southern Italian context of
Maglie and Bari.
1.2 Catholic culture, legal studies, and the University of Bari.
1.3 The Federazione Universitaria Cattolica Italiana (FUCI): Moro's
presidency and his intellectual circle, including his relationship with
Giovanni Battista Montini (the future Paul VI).
1.4 Formation of a juridical and philosophical outlook: Moro as jurist
and his early academic publications.
1.5 Italy under Fascism and the war years as experienced by Moro's
generation of Catholic intellectuals.

### Chapter II — Entry into Politics and the Constituent Assembly,
1943–1948
2.1 The collapse of Fascism and the reorganization of Catholic political
forces; the founding of the Democrazia Cristiana.
2.2 Moro's election to the Constituent Assembly and his role in drafting
the Constitution, with particular attention to his contributions on the
principles later embodied in Articles 1–12.
2.3 Moro's relationship with Alcide De Gasperi and the DC's dominant
faction in the immediate post-war period.
2.4 The 1948 general election and Moro's parliamentary consolidation.

### Chapter III — Parliamentary Rise and Ministerial Apprenticeship,
1948–1959
3.1 Early ministerial posts and their significance for Moro's political
formation.
3.2 Minister of Justice: legislative record and judicial reform debates.
3.3 Minister of Foreign Affairs (1965–1968, cross-referenced forward as
needed) and Moro's early diplomatic instincts, treated here only insofar
as their roots lie in this period.
3.4 The internal architecture of the DC: correnti, patronage networks,
and Moro's own faction ("Base" and successive alignments).

### Chapter IV — Secretary of the Christian Democracy and the "Opening to
the Left," 1959–1968
4.1 Moro's election as DC national secretary and his strategic reading of
post-war Italian society.
4.2 The doctrine of "converging parallels" (*convergenze parallele*) and
the theoretical case for a centre-left alliance with the Italian
Socialist Party (PSI).
4.3 The formation of the first organic centre-left governments and the
resistance from the DC's conservative wing and from industrial and
ecclesiastical interests.
4.4 Domestic reception: trade unions, the Church hierarchy, and the
Italian press.
4.5 International reception, including United States concern over Italian
government participation by parties to the left of the DC.

### Chapter V — Prime Minister: Governing Italy in Turbulent Times, 1963–
1968 and 1974–1976
5.1 The first Moro governments (1963–1968): economic planning, the
"economic miracle's" exhaustion, and social policy.
5.2 The onset of student and worker unrest, 1968–1969 ("l'autunno
caldo"), and the state's response.
5.3 The Piazza Fontana bombing (12 December 1969) and the beginning of
the *anni di piombo* (Years of Lead): Moro's position within the
government's response.
5.4 The second period of Moro governments (1974–1976): economic crisis,
the 1974 divorce referendum, and rising political violence.
5.5 Relations with President Giovanni Leone and with the Bank of Italy
and industrial establishment during economic contraction.

### Chapter VI — Foreign Minister and International Statesman, 1969–1972
and 1973–1974
6.1 Moro's Mediterranean and Middle Eastern diplomacy, including Italy's
posture toward Arab states, the Palestinian question, and reported
informal understandings later associated with the so-called "Lodo Moro."
6.2 Relations with the United States and NATO during a period of
Eurocommunist anxiety.
6.3 Relations with the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc, and Italy's
position within European détente diplomacy.
6.4 Moro's role in early European Community deliberations and his
broader vision of Italy's international alignment.

### Chapter VII — The Historic Compromise: Strategy toward the PCI,
1973–1978
7.1 Enrico Berlinguer's proposal of the *compromesso storico* following
the 1973 Chilean coup, and Moro's independent strategic convergence
toward a comparable design.
7.2 The 1976 general election, the PCI's electoral advance, and the
formation of governments of "national solidarity" (*solidarietà
nazionale*) tolerating or including Communist parliamentary support.
7.3 Moro's election to the presidency of the DC (1976) and his role in
managing the party's relationship with a strengthened PCI.
7.4 Domestic opposition to the strategy from within the DC and from
allied Western governments, including the historiographical debate over
reported United States objections (the contested "Kissinger warning"
episode: present the sourcing and the dispute over its authenticity).
7.5 The state of the historic compromise strategy on the eve of 16 March
1978, including the vote scheduled that day on the government Moro had
worked to construct.

### Chapter VIII — The Kidnapping: Via Fani and the Fifty-Five Days,
16 March – 9 May 1978
8.1 The ambush in Via Fani: sequence of events, the killing of Moro's
five-man escort, and the immediate state response.
8.2 The Red Brigades' Colonna Romana, the "prigione del popolo" in Via
Montalcini, and the organization's internal decision-making as later
reconstructed.
8.3 The "linea della fermezza": the DC's and government's official
non-negotiation position, its principal architects, and its
justification.
8.4 The minority and external positions favouring negotiation, including
the PSI's initiatives, Vatican mediation efforts, and international
back-channel attempts.
8.5 The prison letters: their content, addressees, the disputed question
of Moro's psychological and physical state, and the critical-editorial
history of their publication and authentication.
8.6 Family, Church, and public opinion: the Moro family's public and
private interventions, Pope Paul VI's appeal of 22 April 1978, and the
divided national mood.

### Chapter IX — Death, Investigation, and the Unanswered Questions,
1978 to the present
9.1 The killing of Moro and the discovery of his body in Via Caetani: the
established sequence and its symbolic siting.
9.2 The judicial trials (*Moro-uno* through subsequent proceedings):
verdicts, sentencing, and their evidentiary bases.
9.3 Commissione Moro I (1979–1983): mandate, findings, and limitations.
9.4 Commissione Moro II (2014–2017): new lines of inquiry, forensic
re-examination, and revised or contested conclusions.
9.5 Hypotheses of external involvement: the "Gladio" stay-behind network,
alleged CIA and Soviet-bloc intelligence interest, and the "grandi
vecchi" thesis — presented with due evidentiary weighting per §1.3, not
as established fact.
9.6 Open questions in the historiography as they stand at the time of
writing, including any unresolved forensic, testimonial, or documentary
gaps identified through the cascading research protocol.

### Chapter X — Legacy: Memory, Historiography, and Italy after Moro,
1978 to the present
10.1 Immediate political consequences: the fate of the historic
compromise strategy and the DC's subsequent trajectory.
10.2 The Red Brigades after Moro: organizational fracture, further
actions, and the eventual decline of left-wing terrorism in Italy.
10.3 Institutional memory: memorials, commemorations, archival releases,
and the Moro family's role in shaping public remembrance.
10.4 Cultural and artistic representation: literature, cinema, and public
discourse treating the Moro case, assessed for historiographical
fidelity rather than merely catalogued.
10.5 Historiographical evolution: how interpretation of Moro's project
and death has shifted across five decades of Italian and international
scholarship.
10.6 Aldo Moro's place in the broader history of Cold War Europe and in
Italian democratic self-understanding.

## 3. Editorial and Output Specifications

- **Apparatus.** Provide footnotes or endnotes for every factual claim
  drawn from a specific source; provide a consolidated bibliography
  organized by category (archival/parliamentary, judicial, memoir and
  testimony, secondary historiography, press).
- **Neutrality of register.** Do not adopt the voice of any political
  faction, past or present. Present contested interpretations as
  contested.
- **Internal consistency.** Maintain a single, verified chronology across
  all ten chapters; cross-reference recurring figures and events by
  chapter and subchapter number rather than repeating full context.
- **Length and depth.** Each subchapter should be developed to the depth
  the sourced evidence supports; do not pad with restatement, and do not
  compress a genuinely rich subchapter merely to match the length of its
  neighbours.
- **Language.** Oxford English throughout, per §0.1, applied consistently
  across all ten chapters, the preface, and all apparatus.
- **Appendices.** Include (a) a chronological timeline keyed to chapter
  and subchapter numbers; (b) a dramatis personae; (c) the flagged-claims
  appendix specified in §0.3 if external "Progetto Italia" material was
  supplied; (d) a note on sources consulted versus sources sought but
  unavailable, so that future revision can pick up the cascading research
  protocol where it left off.
